How big is a room?

A plain-English guide to how a house-plan tool decides room sizes, based on what Australian builders actually draw on their published floor plans.

Every size is written as short side × long side, in metres, plus area in m². The figures are room dimensions as printed by the builders. They look like clear internal sizes (wall face to wall face), but the plans never say so, so read them as "as printed". The tool's own sizes are still provisional until they are reviewed against architect-checked plans.

How we worked it out

Where the numbers came from

Two independent sets of published builder plans, all single-storey, 3–4 bedroom detached project homes:

  • A large set: 387 plans from 7 builders. One volume builder supplied 276 of them (71%), covering about 100 designs that are each sold in several sizes.
  • A second set: 53 plans from 7 builders, mostly not that same builder, so it works as a cross-check.

Where the two sets agree, we trust the number more. Where they disagree, this guide says so.

What median, p10 and p90 mean

Line up every room of one type from smallest to largest. If you had 100 bedrooms in a row, the 10th one is the p10 (a compact but real size), the 50th is the median (typical), and the 90th is the p90 (generous but still normal). Percentiles are useful because one odd plan can't drag them around the way an average can.

Each side and each area is summarised separately, so a median area is not always exactly median short side × median long side.

Why we counted per design

The same design is often sold in a smaller and larger version. Counting every plan would let a popular design outvote the rest. So each design contributes one value per side, and the percentiles are taken across designs. The two ways of counting give almost the same answers: the median master is 3.50 × 4.05 per design and 3.59 × 4.18 per plan.

How the tool's minimum, preferred and maximum were chosen

  • Preferred is about the typical (median) size, so the tool aims at what a builder would actually draw.
  • Minimum and maximum start near p10 and p90, then widen where a small set of hand-checked test houses fell outside the range.
  • Garages are a special case. Builders reuse one standard garage, so p10, median and p90 can all be the same number. The garages keep their existing min and max; only the preferred size was moved to match what builders draw.

The main builder never prints a kitchen size, so we added family + dining room by room (33.4 m²). Add a 9.5 m² kitchen and you land close to the 41.6 m² the second set measured directly. Two methods, same answer.

The zone is often not one clean rectangle: L-shapes, stepped shapes, and kitchens tucked into a recess are common. So the tool's numbers describe the zone's bounding rectangle, and it can build an L or stepped shape inside it.

The whole house

A typical footprint is about 12.23 × 21.71 m in the large set and 12.29 × 22.50 m in the second. That's within 6 cm on width and 79 cm on length. The box around the house is about 265 m², but that is not floor area: the builders' own living-area figure has a median of 192 m² (p10 133, p90 325). Including garage and alfresco, total area has a median of 226 m².

Caveats

  • Volume-builder project homes only, not custom architect designs. Two-storey and 5+ bedroom plans were left out.
  • The large set leans on one builder (276 of 387 plans). The second set checks that bias and agrees on the main rooms. Single garages are thin in both (18 and 3 samples).
  • Builders round. Values come to the centimetre or to 10 cm, so the numbers bunch up, and garages often have p10 = median = p90.
  • Internal vs external is unverified. Everything here is "as printed".
  • Whether the master includes its walk-in robe is unknown, and it changes how big the bedroom really is.
  • All the tool's numbers are provisional until they are reviewed against architect-checked plans.
Based on two datasets of published Australian builder floor plans (387 + 53 single-storey, 3–4 bedroom plans). Compiled October 2026.
